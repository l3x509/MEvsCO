They Threatened to Arrest Me for Not Paying a Debt. Is That Legal?

The voice on the phone said if I didn't pay by Friday, they'd "send someone" to pick me up. I froze. Then, once I hung up, I looked it up. Here's exactly what I found out.

M

April 2026 · 8 min read

It was a Tuesday afternoon. I picked up because I didn't recognize the number and thought it might be work. Instead, a voice told me I owed $1,140 on an old account, and if I didn't pay by the end of the week, "the next step is a warrant, and someone will come pick you up." I hung up shaking. I genuinely believed, for about twenty minutes, that I could go to jail over a credit card bill.

Then I did what I do with everything that scares me: I opened my laptop and started searching. What I found out changed the entire way I heard that phone call.

Before we go further

I'm a consumer who went through this, not a lawyer. Nothing here is legal advice. If a collector has threatened you the way I was threatened, the clearest next step is a free consultation with an FDCPA attorney — I'll link to that throughout.

The Verdict: You Cannot Be Arrested for an Unpaid Debt

Illegal. No exceptions for consumer debt.

Failing to pay a credit card, medical bill, personal loan, or most other consumer debt is a civil matter, not a criminal one. You cannot be arrested, jailed, or criminally prosecuted simply for owing money. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act explicitly makes it illegal for a collector to threaten arrest, criminal charges, or jail time as a way to pressure you into paying.

The specific rule is in FDCPA Section 807(4) — part of a broader section that prohibits collectors from making false, deceptive, or misleading representations. Threatening an action that cannot legally be taken counts as exactly that kind of false representation. A collector who tells you that you could be arrested for not paying is not describing a real legal risk. They're describing something that cannot happen under the law as it exists — and telling you it can is itself illegal.

The One Real Exception — and Why It's Not What Collectors Mean

There is a narrow, specific situation where something resembling an arrest can happen in connection with debt — and it's worth understanding clearly, because collectors sometimes lean on the confusion around it.

Contempt of court — a completely different situation

If a creditor has already sued you, won a court judgment, and a judge has ordered you to appear in court — for something like a debtor's examination — and you ignore that court order, a judge can issue a warrant for contempt of court. That warrant is for disobeying the court, not for owing money. It requires an existing lawsuit, a judgment, and a specific order you failed to follow. It is not something a collector can trigger with a phone call, and it is nowhere close to what most arrest threats describe.

When a collector says "pay by Friday or you'll be arrested," they are almost never describing this process — there's no lawsuit, no judgment, no missed court date. They're using the fear of arrest as a shortcut to get you to pay faster. That shortcut is illegal.

"Debt collectors cannot threaten you with arrest or jail for failing to pay a debt. This is one of the most direct violations of consumer protection law."

— Federal Trade Commission, Debt Collection FAQs

Why Collectors Use This Tactic Anyway

Fear of arrest is one of the most effective pressure tactics that exists, precisely because most people don't know the law well enough to recognize it as a bluff. A collector doesn't need every person they call to fall for it — they just need enough people to pay faster than they otherwise would. The tactic is illegal, but it's cheap to use and hard to trace unless the person on the receiving end writes it down.

That's exactly what changes the moment you know this: the threat stops being a reason to panic and becomes a reason to document.

What I Did in the Next 24 Hours

  • Wrote down everything while it was fresh. The date, the time, the exact words used ("send someone to pick you up"), and the number that called. Memory fades fast — I wanted it down before it did.
  • Checked whether I had anything recorded. If a threat like this comes through voicemail or text, save it immediately. It's timestamped evidence in the collector's own words.
  • Did not pay anything under pressure. The fear was designed to make me act fast. I made myself wait.
  • Got a free consultation with an FDCPA attorney. I described exactly what was said. They told me within minutes that it was a likely violation and walked me through what came next — at no cost to me.
  • Filed a complaint with the CFPB and FTC. Both take a few minutes and create an official record, even if enforcement capacity is limited right now.
