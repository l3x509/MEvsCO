Medical Debt and the FDCPA: Special Rules Collectors Hope You Don't Know

A hospital bill went to a collection agency and I assumed the exact same rules applied as with any other debt. Some do. Some don't. Here's what I actually found out, including a credit-report change most people don't know happened.

Medical debt is different from other kinds of debt in a way that isn't obvious until you're dealing with it directly. The bill itself is more likely to be wrong. The rules around your credit report have shifted more than once in the past few years. And the collector calling you may know all of this and count on you not knowing it too.

Before we go further

I'm a consumer who researched this, not a lawyer. Nothing here is legal advice. If a medical debt collector is pursuing you, a free consultation with an FDCPA attorney is the clearest next step.

The FDCPA Applies — Once It's With a Collector

Once a medical bill has been sent to a third-party collection agency, every standard FDCPA protection applies exactly as it would with a credit card or personal loan debt: no calls before 8am or after 9pm, no threats of arrest, no harassment, no lying about the amount owed, and the right to request debt validation. The one distinction worth knowing is that the FDCPA generally doesn't cover the original creditor — meaning if the hospital or provider itself is calling you directly, rather than a separate collection agency, those specific protections may not apply the same way.

Why Medical Bills Are Wrong More Often

This is the part that surprised me most. Medical billing runs through providers, insurers, and multiple layers of coding, and each layer is a chance for an error — incorrect patient matching, insurance payments that were never properly credited, duplicate charges, or bills for services never actually received.

Don't pay without validation

Never pay a medical debt in collections without first requesting a debt validation letter and comparing it against your own records and your insurance explanation of benefits. Given how error-prone medical billing is, validation isn't optional caution — it's a basic first step.

Medical Debt and Your Credit Report

This area has shifted a lot recently, and it's worth being precise about where things actually stand. The three major credit bureaus voluntarily changed their policies in 2022 and 2023 to stop reporting medical debt collections under $500, and to stop reporting paid medical collections regardless of amount. Those voluntary changes are still in effect.

The CFPB separately finalized a rule in January 2025 that would have banned nearly all medical debt from credit reports. That rule never fully took hold — a federal court vacated it entirely in July 2025, at the CFPB's own request following the change in administration. As of today, there is no federal ban on medical debt appearing on credit reports.

What about state-level bans?

Several states passed their own laws banning medical debt from credit reports. The same court ruling that struck down the federal rule also raised questions about whether federal law preempts those state bans — and that fight is still being litigated. If you live in a state with one of these laws, don't assume it's a guaranteed protection right now; the legal status is genuinely unsettled.

If Insurance Was Supposed to Pay

If a bill went to collections for an amount your insurance was supposed to cover, that's a billing dispute, not simply an unpaid debt. Involve your insurance company directly, request validation from the collector, and avoid making any payment while the coverage question is unresolved — paying can look like accepting the debt as entirely your own responsibility.

What I'd Do With a Medical Collection Account

  • Request debt validation before paying anything. Ask for an itemized breakdown including the provider, date of service, and what was billed, and compare it against your own records.
  • Contact your insurance company if any portion should have been covered, before making any payment to the collector.
  • Check your state's current medical debt credit reporting rules — but don't assume a state ban is fully enforceable right now, given the ongoing legal challenge.
  • Document every call from the collector the same way you would for any other debt — the FDCPA's protections apply in full once it's with a third-party agency.
  • Get a free consultation with an FDCPA attorney if anything about how they're collecting feels wrong — medical debt disputes and FDCPA violations come up together often.
